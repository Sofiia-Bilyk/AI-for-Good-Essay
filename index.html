When does artificial intelligence actually make the world better?

01 / A place that stays with you

During my two semesters in Japan, I spent a week in Kamaishi, a coastal city devastated by the 2011 earthquake and tsunami. I experienced several earthquakes there. Although none was particularly destructive, they made me think about life with the constant possibility of a natural disaster.

No one knows exactly when the next major earthquake will strike, but the minutes following it can determine whether people are safe.

02 / What could a few minutes change?

I wonder how AI could help communities like Kamaishi become more resilient. While AI cannot reliably predict when an earthquake will occur, it could help analyze geographic data, simulate different tsunami scenarios, and optimize evacuation routes.

If an AI system could help a community evacuate even a few minutes earlier, its contribution could be extraordinary.

03 / Two perspectives on technology

Another reflection I had there was about our relationship with technology. Living in San Francisco, I observe AI becoming the default solution to almost everything, from everyday tasks to complex professional decisions.

In Japan, I encountered a more cautious approach to adopting AI. Can a more selective approach sometimes produce better outcomes? I don't believe the answer is simply to embrace AI everywhere or to resist it.

I believe we should be much more deliberate about identifying where it creates genuine value.

04 / From observation to building

I encountered a similar question while co-founding a wildfire intelligence platform, Fuego.Earth ↗. During interviews, firefighters explained that existing fire-spread simulations could take too long, limiting their usefulness when decisions had to be made within minutes.

This made me realize that just because advanced technology exists doesn't necessarily mean people benefit from it. That insight led my co-founders and me to develop a prevention-focused wildfire intelligence tool that provides professionals with actionable insights in just a few minutes.

05 / The hidden costs

These experiences strengthened my belief that AI is a powerful tool for social good. But they also raised another question:

What if an AI solution intended to protect the environment ultimately causes more environmental damage than the benefit it creates?

Power, water, materials, and computing infrastructure belong in the equation.

06 / A proposal

To address this dilemma, I propose exploring the creation of an independent international AI Net Impact Assessment Alliance, dedicated to evaluating whether AI applications generate meaningful social and environmental benefits relative to their costs.

07 / A shared method

The alliance could bring together researchers from institutions such as Tsinghua University, sustainability organizations such as the Green Software Foundation, technology companies, policymakers, and local communities.

Working together, they could establish transparent methods that compare AI with realistic alternatives, including non-AI approaches. Assessments would consider hardware manufacturing, electricity, emissions, and water alongside measurable benefits such as resource conservation and improved disaster response.

Researchers would develop and validate methods.

Governments and communities would test whether those methods hold up in practice.

08 / Influence creates responsibility

However, I believe it would be especially important to involve the companies developing frontier AI, such as OpenAI and Anthropic.

As pioneers shaping the development and widespread adoption of AI, these companies have both the influence and responsibility to ensure the technology evolves sustainably and increases transparency around resource consumption. Beyond technical expertise, they could also contribute financial resources. As Nan Ransohoff discusses in The Third Wave of American Philanthropy ↗, AI-generated wealth could create unprecedented opportunities for public benefit, reflected in the OpenAI Foundation and philanthropic commitments from Anthropic's founders. Such support could help make environmental assessments accessible worldwide, while an independent association oversees assessments to ensure objective evaluation.

09 / Back to Kamaishi

I think back to Kamaishi. If AI could help people prepare for the next tsunami, that would be an extraordinary application of technology.

But the achievement would not be the AI system itself. It would be the people who reach safety because it helped them make better decisions.

And if another solution could achieve the same result more reliably, accessibly, and sustainably, we should be just as willing to choose it.

AI for Good begins with a better question.
